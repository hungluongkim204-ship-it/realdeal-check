Black Friday UK vs US: Same Name, Different Shopping Season

Black Friday started in the United States. The UK imported the sales event, not the holiday — and that difference shapes how each country shops it.

The timing difference that matters: Thanksgiving

In the US, Black Friday is anchored to Thanksgiving, which falls on the fourth Thursday of November. That pins Black Friday to a fixed spot (November 27 in 2026) and gives the whole weekend a rhythm: Thanksgiving Day sales, Black Friday, Small Business Saturday, then Cyber Monday on the following Monday. Adobe’s 2026 forecast expects sporting goods to peak on Thanksgiving itself, TVs, toys, apparel, appliances and furniture on Black Friday, and electronics and computers on Cyber Monday.

In the UK, there’s no Thanksgiving, so Black Friday is simply a date-driven sales event adopted from the US. It lands on the same Friday, but there’s no bank holiday and no four-day weekend — most people are at work, so UK Black Friday is far more online. Retailers have stretched it into “Black Friday week” or even a fortnight-long promotion rather than a one-day rush.

Practical consequence for UK shoppers: don’t expect sharp price movements on a single day. Expect a slow descent over two weeks — which makes the 90-day low test even more useful, because the “deadline” pricing pressure is weaker than in the US.

Consumer protection basics

Both countries have established frameworks, and knowing the basics changes how you shop a sale:

  • United States: the FTC enforces truth-in-advertising rules — a “was” price should reflect a real, recent price — but enforcement is complaint-driven. Credit cards offer dispute rights for goods that never arrive or aren’t as described. Return policies vary by retailer.
  • United Kingdom: the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives a statutory right to a refund within 30 days for faulty goods, and Consumer Contracts Regulations give most online purchases a 14-day change-of-mind return window. Exceptions apply, and this does not automatically apply to items bought in store.

In both countries, one principle holds: a discount claim should be judged against a real earlier price. The crossed-out “was” price is set by the seller; the 90-day price history is not.

What the UK has that the US doesn’t: Boxing Day

Boxing Day, December 26, is the UK’s traditional clearance kickoff — a bank holiday when retailers start shifting Christmas stock, and for many retailers the start of their biggest markdown period of the year. It flows straight into January sales, which run through roughly the first two weeks of January.

The US has after-Christmas sales too, but Boxing Day carries no comparable cultural weight. For UK shoppers, this extends the deal calendar a full three weeks past Black Friday — with the same caveat: clearance means old stock at low prices, not automatically the best-ever price. Compare against the 90-day low before assuming a clearance sticker is a bargain.

One more UK date worth knowing: Green Monday, December 14, 2026 — the second Monday in December, historically near the last window for online orders to arrive before Christmas.

The verdict: same test, two calendars

Whether you’re shopping the US Thanksgiving weekend or the UK’s Boxing Day clearance, the mechanics of a fake discount don’t change. Check the price history, compare against the 90-day low, and let the calendar tell you when your category tends to bottom out — not whether the number in front of you is real.
