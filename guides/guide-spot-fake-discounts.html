How to Spot a Fake Discount: A Step-by-Step Method

Most “deals” aren’t deals. You don’t need to be an expert to catch most fake discounts — you need a method.

When UK consumer group Which? tracked Black Friday promotions, it found that 9 in 10 tracked products were the same price or cheaper in the six months before the event. Surveys point the same direction: only 54% of shoppers trust that Black Friday prices are genuinely lower (BCG, Nov 2026), and 59% believe retailers raise prices in the weeks before the sale (Omni Calculator survey, 2026).

Step 1: Ignore the crossed-out price completely

The “was £499, now £349” line is the least trustworthy number on the page. Retailers can set the “was” price themselves — a common tactic is to list an item at an inflated price for a few weeks so the sale has something dramatic to compare against.

Your real reference point is the 90-day low: the lowest price the item actually sold for over the last three months. If the deal price isn’t meaningfully below that, the discount banner is decoration.

Step 2: Look up the price history

Use a free price-history tool to find the 90-day low:

  • Amazon products: CamelCamelCamel or Keepa.
  • UK high-street and multi-retailer items: PriceSpy or PriceRunner.
  • Google Shopping: price history for many products in the shopping panel.

Take the lowest price in the chart from the past 90 days and write it down.

Step 3: Check the model number

Some products are manufactured in special Black Friday-only variants — same brand, similar name, slightly different model number. These were never sold before the event, so there’s no price history to compare against, and “50% off” is anchored against a price nobody ever paid.

Before you buy, check whether the exact model number appears in price history before the sale season started. If the product has no history, treat any percentage-off claim as unverifiable.

Step 4: Decode the “up to X% off” language

“Up to 70% off” is legally different from “70% off”. It means at least one item somewhere in the range hits that number — possibly clearance stock of a discontinued colour. Judge the specific item in front of you, not the headline.

Step 5: Watch the urgency mechanics

Countdown timers and “only 3 left” banners exist to stop you checking. Which? has repeatedly found that the same prices return after the event, which means the deadline was never real. The rule of thumb: the more pressure the page applies, the more important it is to verify the price history before buying.

Step 6: Factor in bundles and accessories

Bundles make price comparison harder because there’s no clean history for the bundle itself. If a bundle looks cheap, price each component separately against its own 90-day low and add them up. Sometimes the bundle saves you money; sometimes it sells slow-moving accessories at full price alongside a discounted hero item.

A worked example

Say you’re looking at wireless headphones listed at $349, “was $499”.

  1. You open CamelCamelCamel and see the item sold at $329 for most of September and October, before creeping up to $499 in early November.
  2. The real comparison is $349 vs. the 90-day low of $329 — the “deal” is actually $20 more expensive than the earlier price. Verdict: normal price dressed up as a sale.
  3. You also notice the model number ends in a suffix that only appears in November. Even without the chart, that is a red flag.

If the same headphones were listed at $299, that would be below the $329 low — a genuine drop, and worth buying if you actually need them.
